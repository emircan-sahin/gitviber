Why tide pools glow at night

On a moonless night along the northern coast, the shallow pools left behind by the tide begin to shimmer with a cold blue light. The glow comes from dinoflagellates, single-celled plankton that flash whenever the water around them is disturbed.1

Researchers have spent decades trying to understand why these organisms spend energy on light. The leading idea is a burglar alarm: a flash startles grazers and draws in the predators that hunt them, so the plankton survive the encounter more often than not.

The light itself is made by a reaction between luciferin and the enzyme luciferase, packed into tiny compartments called scintillons. A change in acidity, set off by the movement of the water, opens the reaction for a fraction of a second, long enough for a visible spark.

Not every flash is the same. Some species glow for longer, and some only at certain hours, keeping a clock that runs even in a dark laboratory. Read the study to see how the rhythm was measured over many weeks.

light = luciferin + O2 → oxyluciferin + photon

It looks like the stars fell into the water and are trying to get back out.

For visitors, the best time to see the glow is a few nights after a new moon, on a calm evening when the water is warm. Stir a pool gently with your hand and watch the sparks trail behind your fingers.

1. Field notes from the coastal survey, recorded over three summers by the team.
